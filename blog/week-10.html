Week 10: Repeated Intent Decomposition: Handling Duplicate Intents in Compound Utterances

VT
VITA Engineering Team
• March 9, 2026 • 18 min read

In Week 9, we achieved a milestone in VITA’s natural language pipeline: training our single-intent BERT classifier to 98.6% Macro F1, conquering CUDA driver mismatches, and executing calibrated inference in 4.15ms on ARM CPU. Our device could effortlessly classify queries like “How much protein is in 100g of salmon?” and route them deterministically to CIQUAL.

However, when we put VITA into early human speech testing, real users rarely spoke like clean unit tests. Instead of asking one isolated question, users routinely compound their requests into a single continuous breath:

Real-World Compound Utterance

“How many calories are in 100 grams of Atlantic salmon, and how much protein is in 200 grams of chicken breast?”

When this utterance hit our Week 9 engine, the system experienced a fatal linguistic failure. Both halves of the sentence represent the exact same intent (food_composition_lookup), but they target two distinct food entities with different gram weights and different nutrient targets. Because classical multi-label classifiers output a mathematical set of active classes, the duplicate intent collapsed into a single label, silently discarding the second food. This week, we chronicle how we solved the repeated same-intent problem through clause-level span segmentation, a 12-rule semantic dataset contract, and rigorous training set engineering.

The Compound Utterance Dilemma: Set-Theoretic Collapse

To understand why this problem broke traditional NLU architectures, consider how typical intent classification models operate at document or sentence level:

  • Multi-Class Softmax: Constrains the output distribution to Σ pi = 1.0. Given two intents in one sentence, it arbitrarily crowns one winner (e.g. salmon) and completely ignores the second (chicken).
  • Multi-Label Sigmoid: Outputs independent binary probabilities for each label in the taxonomy ∈ {0, 1}C. For our query, the vector activates bit food_composition_lookup = 1.0.

Here lies the mathematical trap: in set theory, duplicate elements are idempotent:

{ food_composition_lookup, food_composition_lookup } ≡ { food_composition_lookup }

A multi-label classification vector cannot represent multiplicity. It registers that food_composition_lookup is present, but it cannot convey that it is present twice. Even worse, it divorces the intent from its textual span: our downstream orchestration engine was left blind to which tokens belonged to salmon and which belonged to chicken breast.

The Three Query Archetypes: Split vs. Merge Adjudication

The core linguistic insight that guided our architecture was recognizing that not all multi-item nutrition queries should be decomposed. There are three distinct query structures that a voice assistant must differentiate:

Query Archetype Example User Utterance Linguistic Structure Target Disposition Adjudication Rule
Pattern A: Multi-Attribute Query “What are the calories, carbohydrates, and protein in 100g of salmon?” 1 Food Entity • Multiple Target Nutrients K = 1 Merged Instance
1 SQL query returning multiple column values.		 Rule 4 (Merge Consistency): Same intent + identical food entity MUST merge.
Pattern B: Comparative Query “Which has more protein: Atlantic salmon or chicken breast?” Multiple Foods • 1 Comparative Predicate (“vs”, “more”) K = 1 Comparative Instance
Dispatched as a relational comparison payload.		 Rule 5 (Comparative Adjudication): Single comparative predicate scopes across both entities.
Pattern C: Repeated Same-Intent “What are the calories in salmon, and what is the protein in chicken?” Multiple Distinct Foods • Independent Clauses K = 2 Distinct Instances
Rank 1: Salmon lookup; Rank 2: Chicken lookup.		 Rule 6 (Independent Adjudication): Coordinated lookups across distinct entities preserve separate instances.

Notice how easy it would be for a naive model to make mistakes: if a system splits on every conjunction (“and”), it will erroneously fragment Pattern A into three separate queries (“What are the calories”, “carbohydrates”, and “protein in salmon”). Conversely, if it refuses to split same-intent sentences, it will drop the second food in Pattern C. Solving this required strict mathematical definitions of communicative units.

Figure 2: The deterministic decision logic for adjudicating compound multi-nutrient and multi-entity utterances.

Architecture: Compositional Decomposition & Clause Grounding

Rather than retraining a massive monolithic transformer that tries to simultaneously parse token syntax and classify entire documents, we adopted a two-stage factorized architecture:

  1. Communicative Clause Span Segmentation: A lightweight segmenter identifies communicative discourse boundaries (conjunctions, semicolons, conversational pivots) and emits bounded ClauseSpan objects with verbatim character slices and offsets.
  2. Atomic Intent & Entity Grounding: Each bounded span is fed into our verified BERT backbone, extracting its sub-intent and grounding recognized food nouns against the canonical integer primary keys (alim_code) in database/ciqual.db.
  3. Merge & Adjudication Engine: The candidate instances are passed through our 12 semantic rules to decide whether instances should be merged (Rule 4), structured as a comparison (Rule 5), or dispatched sequentially as independent queries (Rule 6).

The Core Domain Dataclasses

In our production codebase (classifier/schema_types/multi_intent.py), every communicative act is formalized into two immutable structures:

from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Tuple, Optional

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class ClauseSpan:
    """Verbatim text slice and exact character offsets within the original utterance."""
    start: int
    end: int
    text: str

    def __post_init__(self) -> None:
        if self.start < 0 or self.end < self.start:
            raise ValueError(f"Invalid span bounds: [{self.start}:{self.end}]")
        if len(self.text) != (self.end - self.start):
            raise ValueError(
                f"Span text length ({len(self.text)}) != delta {self.end - self.start}"
            )

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class IntentInstance:
    """The authoritative semantic unit of execution in VITA."""
    rank: int                                      # Appearance order (1-indexed: 1..N)
    clause_span: ClauseSpan                        # Exact character slice in original query
    global_category: str                          # Level 1 taxonomy: ciqual, user_profile, meta, none
    sub_intent: Optional[str]                     # Level 2 taxonomy: food_composition_lookup, etc.
    entities: Tuple[str, ...] = field(default_factory=tuple)
    canonical_entity_ids: Tuple[int, ...] = field(default_factory=tuple) # CIQUAL alim_code
    confidence: float = 1.0
    dispatch: bool = True

    @property
    def merge_key_symmetric(self) -> Tuple[Optional[str], frozenset]:
        """Symmetric signature used for duplicate detection under Rule 3."""
        return (self.sub_intent, frozenset(self.canonical_entity_ids))

The 12-Rule Semantic Dataset Contract

Before generating a single training sample or benchmark file, we codified Phase 3.1A with an invariant: “Do not generate compound training samples until the schema validator can reject semantically inconsistent annotations.”

Implemented in classifier/dataset/multi_intent_validator.py, the validator enforces 12 immutable rules on every sample:

  • Rule 1 (Complexity Overflow): Total communicative acts K ≤ 3. If K > 3, the query fails closed with EXCESSIVE_COMPLEXITY and dispatch=False to protect edge memory.
  • Rule 2 (Cardinality Consistency): detected_intent_count must equal the exact sum of dispatched, unsupported, and blocked clauses.
  • Rule 3 (Duplicate Intent Detection & Rank Continuity): Rejects redundant identical symmetric keys (sub_intent, frozenset(canonical_entity_ids)) and asserts continuous 1-indexed ranks (1..N).
  • Rule 4 (Merge Consistency): Instances sharing the same sub-intent and identical food entities must be merged into K = 1 multi-attribute lookups.
  • Rule 5 (Comparative Adjudication): Prohibits fragmenting comparative queries (“compare”, “versus”, “which has more”) into multiple independent lookups.
  • Rule 6 (Independent Request Adjudication): Enforces that independent queries across distinct entities remain distinct IntentInstance records.
  • Rule 7 (Database Grounding): Asserts that all entity IDs exist in database/ciqual.db (foods.alim_code).
  • Rule 8 (Coreference State Validity): Rejects ambiguous antecedents from dispatching unless explicitly resolved.
  • Rule 9 (Dispatch Safety Guard): Enforces dispatch=False on unsupported or blocked clauses.
  • Rule 10 (Conservation of Clauses): Zero dropped clauses: Total Communicative Acts ≡ Dispatched + Unsupported + Blocked.
  • Rule 11 (Character Slicing Integrity): Asserts verbatim byte-for-byte slicing: sample.text[span.start : span.end] == span.text.
  • Rule 12 (Zero Hallucinated Clauses): Prohibits any span text that does not exist within the original user text.

Live Code: Validating Rules 3, 4, and 11

Here is how the validator prevents data corruption in classifier/dataset/multi_intent_validator.py:

# Rule 3: Duplicate Intent Detection & Rank Continuity
seen_symmetric_keys = set()
ranks = []
for inst in sample.intents:
    ranks.append(inst.rank)
    key = inst.merge_key_symmetric
    if key in seen_symmetric_keys and len(inst.canonical_entity_ids) > 0:
        errors.append(
            f"Rule 3 Violation (Duplicate Intent): Found duplicate instance with "
            f"sub_intent='{inst.sub_intent}' and entities={inst.canonical_entity_ids}"
        )
    seen_symmetric_keys.add(key)

if ranks and ranks != list(range(1, len(ranks) + 1)):
    errors.append(f"Rule 3 Violation (Rank Continuity): Expected 1..{len(ranks)}, got {ranks}")

# Rule 4: Merge Consistency (Unmerged same-food instances)
for idx1 in range(len(sample.intents)):
    for idx2 in range(idx1 + 1, len(sample.intents)):
        inst1, inst2 = sample.intents[idx1], sample.intents[idx2]
        if (
            inst1.sub_intent == inst2.sub_intent
            and inst1.canonical_entity_ids
            and inst2.canonical_entity_ids
            and set(inst1.canonical_entity_ids) == set(inst2.canonical_entity_ids)
        ):
            errors.append(
                f"Rule 4 Violation (Merge Inconsistency): Ranks {inst1.rank} and {inst2.rank} "
                f"share sub_intent '{inst1.sub_intent}' and identical entity IDs "
                f"{inst1.canonical_entity_ids} but were not merged."
            )

# Rule 11: Character Slicing Integrity
for span in all_spans:
    sliced_text = sample.text[span.start : span.end]
    if sliced_text != span.text:
        errors.append(
            f"Rule 11 Violation (Slicing Mismatch): At [{span.start}:{span.end}], "
            f"expected '{sliced_text}', but span.text is '{span.text}'"
        )

Training Set Engineering: Building Repeated Same-Intent Samples

With the 12-rule contract enforced, we constructed our compound training dataset in classifier/dataset/build_multi_intent_dataset.py. Synthesizing repeated same-intent samples requires far more precision than simple string concatenation:

1. Offset-Preserving Synthetic Synthesis

When stitching two single-intent clause templates together with natural discourse connectors (“, and ”, “ and also ”, “; also ”, “, and tell me ”), the character start and end offsets of the second clause must be calculated with zero drift:

# Synthesizing Repeated Same-Intent Clauses with Zero Character Drift
food_id_1, food_id_2 = rng.sample(food_ids, 2)
f1_name = _clean_food_name(foods[str(food_id_1)]["name_en"])
f2_name = _clean_food_name(foods[str(food_id_2)]["name_en"])

tmpl1, _ = rng.choice(COMPOSITION_CLAUSES)
tmpl2, _ = rng.choice(COMPOSITION_CLAUSES)

c1_text = tmpl1.format(food=f1_name)
c2_text = tmpl2.format(food=f2_name)
connector = rng.choice([", and ", " and also ", "; also "])

# Full compound utterance text
full_text = f"{c1_text}{connector}{c2_text}"

# Exact mathematical character offset calculation
span1 = ClauseSpan(start=0, end=len(c1_text), text=c1_text)
c2_start = len(c1_text) + len(connector)
c2_end = c2_start + len(c2_text)
span2 = ClauseSpan(start=c2_start, end=c2_end, text=c2_text)

# Two distinct IntentInstance records under the same sub_intent
inst1 = IntentInstance(rank=1, clause_span=span1, global_category="ciqual",
                       sub_intent="food_composition_lookup",
                       entities=(f1_name,), canonical_entity_ids=(food_id_1,))
inst2 = IntentInstance(rank=2, clause_span=span2, global_category="ciqual",
                       sub_intent="food_composition_lookup",
                       entities=(f2_name,), canonical_entity_ids=(food_id_2,))

2. Hard Negative Training Slices (Tier 3)

To prevent the classifier from over-segmenting, we engineered 3,000 Tier 3 Hard Negatives:

  • 1,200 Multi-Attribute Negatives: Queries like “What are the calories, carbohydrates, and protein in 100g of Atlantic salmon?” contains coordinating conjunctions (“and”), but targets only one food. They must evaluate to K = 1.
  • 1,000 Comparative Negatives: Queries like “Compare the protein and fat content in 100g of salmon versus 100g of ribeye steak” contains two foods, but a single comparative predicate. They must evaluate to K = 1.
  • 800 Compound Syntax Negatives: Queries like “Look in the database and tell me if eggs have gluten” contain two verbs (look, tell), but only one intent.

3. Zero-Leakage Cryptographic Entity Isolation

To ensure evaluation purity, we implemented an isolation checker (classifier/dataset/isolation_checker.py) that verifies entity sets, pairs, and triples across splits on canonical integer IDs:

Entities(Train) ∩ Entities(Val) ≡ ∅      Entities(Train) ∩ Entities(Cal) ≡ ∅

If any food entity appears in both training and validation/canary splits, compilation immediately aborts with an AssertionError.

Looking Ahead to Week 11

This week, we mastered handling repeated occurrences of the same intent in a compound sentence. We can now effortlessly decompose a query about two different foods and execute independent database lookups.

However, human conversation can combine completely disparate tasks in a single sentence: “What are the calories in salmon, does it have gluten, and can you log my morning weight at 78kg?” That sentence spans three completely different domains: food composition (CIQUAL), allergens, and user profile management.
