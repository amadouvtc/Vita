Week 3: Voice In, Voice Out: From Signal Processing to Local Neural STT & TTS

Welcome to Week 3 of building VITA! After wiring up our Raspberry Pi 4's 40-pin GPIO header and proving out our I2S audio interfaces last week, our goal for this week was simple on paper: make the hardware speak and listen. But we weren't ready for databases yet—we were still neck-deep on the hardware bench.

Bare-Metal Architecture: No Containers on the Bench

An important architectural milestone to clarify: in Week 3, we were running everything directly on the Linux host—zero Docker, zero containers. We were executing bare-metal Python and C routines directly on 64-bit Raspberry Pi OS Lite, binding directly to ALSA kernel memory mappings (i2s-mmap) and the host ARM Cortex-A72 thread scheduler. Before introducing containerization overhead, we needed to know what the bare hardware was truly capable of.

Our journey this week unfolded across three dramatic technical phases: from pure classical digital signal processing (DSP) which failed miserably, through a temporary cloud API baseline that worked but violated our core privacy mission, to a genuine breakthrough: running production-grade neural STT and TTS models directly on the host Raspberry Pi 4.

The Three Architectural Phases We Tested

Building an autonomous voice device from scratch forces you to confront the trade-offs between compute overhead, acoustic accuracy, and user privacy. Here is the evolution of how we engineered VITA's voice stack in Week 3:

Figure 1: The three architectural phases evaluated in Week 3, culminating in bare-metal local neural execution on the Raspberry Pi 4 host.

Phase 1: Pure Signal Processing & Why It Failed

Because running deep neural networks on embedded microprocessors is notoriously compute-heavy, our initial engineering reflex was to attempt classical digital signal processing (DSP). We asked: Can we avoid neural networks entirely and use fast algorithmic heuristics?

We wrote a bare-metal Python script utilizing scipy and numpy directly on the Pi 4 host to process audio captured from the INMP441 I2S microphone array:

  • Speech-to-Text via Heuristic DSP: We implemented Short-Time Fourier Transforms (STFT), Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs), and Zero-Crossing Rate (ZCR) energy detectors. We attempted Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) to match acoustic envelopes against stored templates of food keywords ("apple", "grams", "calories").
  • Text-to-Speech via Formant Synthesis: For voice output, we used classical formant resonators (modulating F1, F2, and F3 vocal-tract resonances) combined with diphone concatenative stitching, similar to legacy formant engines like eSpeak.

What Happened: The Acoustic Disaster

The result was catastrophic on both ends:

1. STT was virtually unusable: The DSP heuristics dropped consonants, missed words, and completely collapsed in our kitchen testing. When an exhaust fan was running or a pan was sizzling in the background, ambient white noise completely overwhelmed the zero-crossing detector. Word recognition accuracy was a pathetic 18.4%.

2. TTS sounded like a 1980s Speak & Spell: Because concatenative and formant synthesis lack phase alignment between phonemes, every transition created a sharp, audible click—what our team called "stitched sounds". The voice was metallic, harsh, and completely alienating. Nobody would want this device sitting in their home speaking to them.

Phase 2: The Cloud API Baseline Pivot

Frustrated by the acoustic failure of classical DSP, we decided to put pure algorithmic signal processing aside. We needed to prove out the interaction loop and establish a quality benchmark: What does a responsive, natural conversational health assistant actually feel like?

We built a temporary bridge running directly on the Raspberry Pi 4 host that connected our hardware I/O to external cloud APIs:

  1. Audio Capture: The Pi recorded the entire user utterance over the I2S microphone array into a local 16 kHz 16-bit WAV buffer in host RAM.
  2. Cloud STT POST: The Pi made an HTTPS POST request transmitting the WAV file to an external cloud speech-to-text API, which returned the recognized text transcript.
  3. Cloud TTS Streaming: The Pi posted the response text string to a cloud voice provider, streamed back raw audio bytes, and piped them directly to aplay through the MAX98357A I2S amplifier.
scripts/voice_pipeline_benchmarks/02_cloud_api_bridge_prototype.py Python 
# Step 1: Capture whole utterance on host into local WAV buffer
input_wav = record_device_audio(duration_sec=3)

# Step 2: POST audio payload to cloud speech provider

What Happened: Great Sound, Fatal Privacy Flaw

The cloud bridge worked significantly better: transcription accuracy skyrocketed to 99.1%, and the synthesized speech was warm, melodic, and human. For the first time, our hardware bench felt like magic.

However, it violated the core philosophical pillar of VITA: Data Sovereignty.

Sending private audio recorded inside a user's home across the public internet to third-party servers meant users had to trust external corporations with their intimate conversations. Furthermore, round-trip latency varied wildly depending on Wi-Fi connection strength, introducing 2.1 to 3.8 seconds of awkward silence before the device answered. We knew we could not ship this.

Phase 3: The Breakthrough: Local Neural Models on the Host

The question was now existential: Can we match the accuracy and warmth of modern neural models, but execute them 100% offline directly on the Raspberry Pi 4's ARM processor?

The answer was yes. By leveraging the latest breakthroughs in model quantization and ONNX execution, we successfully ported and validated local neural STT and TTS engines directly on the bare-metal Linux host.

Figure 2: VITA's bare-metal neural voice topology running on the Raspberry Pi 4 host without containers.

1. On-Device STT: faster-whisper with CTranslate2 INT8

Rather than running stock PyTorch Whisper (which consumes over 1.8 GB RAM and struggles with ARM CPU inference), we deployed faster-whisper based on CTranslate2 directly in a host Python virtualenv. By quantizing weights to INT8 and compiling with ARM NEON vector instructions, the model was lean enough to run directly on the Broadcom BCM2711:

Host Bare-Metal STT: faster-whisper Python 
from faster_whisper import WhisperModel

# Initialized directly on host memory — no container boundaries
model = WhisperModel(
    model_size_or_path="tiny.en",
    device="cpu",
    compute_type="int8",
    cpu_threads=4,          # Saturates all 4 Cortex-A72 cores
    num_workers=1
)

# Built-in WebRTC VAD skips silence and prevents hallucination
segments, info = model.transcribe(
    "audio_utterance.wav",
    beam_size=1,            # Greedy decoding for lowest latency
    vad_filter=True,
    language="en"
)

transcript = " ".join([seg.text for seg in segments]).strip()
# Transcribes a 3-second food utterance in just ~0.95s on the Pi 4!

2. On-Device TTS: Piper Neural Voice via ONNX Runtime

For voice generation, we turned to Piper, an ultra-fast, local neural text-to-speech engine running on ONNX Runtime CPU. Piper synthesizes high-fidelity 22.05 kHz audio from raw phonemes in a fraction of a second without any of the jarring clicks or robotic stitches of formant synthesis:

Host Bare-Metal TTS: Piper ONNX Python 
import onnxruntime as ort
import wave

# Pre-warmed ONNX session in host RAM
session_opts = ort.SessionOptions()
session_opts.intra_op_num_threads = 4
piper_model = ort.InferenceSession("en_US-lessac-medium.onnx", session_opts)

# Synthesizes speech locally in ~380ms with natural inflection
wav_bytes = synthesize_speech(piper_model, "Logged 150 grams of salmon.")

# Play directly to the I2S speaker using ALSA aplay
with open("/tmp/reply.wav", "wb") as f:
    f.write(wav_bytes)
os.system("aplay -D default -q /tmp/reply.wav")

Week 3 Wrap-Up & Looking Ahead to Week 4

Week 3 ended with a triumphant breakthrough on our hardware bench. We proved that we do not need to compromise on VITA's core ethos: we do not need the cloud to have a smart, articulate voice assistant. By running faster-whisper and piper-tts directly on the bare-metal Raspberry Pi 4 host, we achieved:

  • Crystal-clear voice recognition without dropping syllables in noisy kitchen acoustics.
  • Warm, natural speech synthesis completely free of the jarring stitches and metallic buzz of legacy formant engines.
  • Sub-1.5 second conversational turnaround, running entirely offline with zero container virtualization overhead.
  • Absolute data sovereignty: audio waveforms never leave the device.

With our Raspberry Pi 4 now equipped with both ears and a voice, our hardware can officially communicate with humans. Now, it needs a brain that can understand human language and formulate coherent thoughts. Next week in Week 4, we will explore Large Language Models (LLMs) to bring true conversational intelligence to the device.
