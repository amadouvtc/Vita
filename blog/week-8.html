Week 8: Eliminating Probabilistic Drift: The BERT Intent Classifier and Auditable Voice Pipeline

In Week 7, we achieved an engineering milestone: we compiled and federated four authoritative European health and environmental databases into an immutable, 22MB SQLite knowledge base. Our Orange Pi 5 could calculate carbon footprints, look up 74 macro- and micronutrients, verify statutory allergens, and evaluate oral allergy cross-reactions in under 3.3 milliseconds.

Yet as we hooked this multi-database engine into our voice loop, we slammed into a catastrophic safety issue. Because we relied on our local language model to decide which intent to execute, we decided to run an automated stress test: we sent the exact same transcribed utterance 1,000 times in a row to see if the LLM would always select the same database query.

It didn't.

The 1,000-Trial Non-Determinism Failure

Testing the query "What is the range of fat content in avocados?" across 1,000 consecutive identical invocations, our local LLM router failed 96 times (a 9.6% error rate). In 57 runs, it drifted into data_trust_confidence_codes; in 23 runs, it routed to conversational meta-query; and in 16 runs, it completely failed into an ungrounded fallback. Even at low temperature settings, token sampling and prompt attention drift produced stochastic jitter.

For a casual chatbot, a 9% routing drift is an annoyance. For an edge device offering clinical dietary and allergen protection, non-deterministic routing is completely unacceptable. If an allergic user asks if pesto contains nuts, the system cannot pick the allergen database 91% of the time and drift into generic conversational chat the other 9% of the time. VITA must be 100% auditable: every action, query, and parameter must trace back to a mathematically deterministic reason.

Furthermore, injecting dozens of database schemas, descriptions, and routing rules into the system prompt caused massive prompt bloat, exhausting our local Qwen 2.5 1.5B's context window and degrading inference speed on the RK3588S. We reached an undeniable conclusion: the language model must never be the intent router.

The New Paradigm: Dedicated Intent Classification

We pivoted to a fundamentally different paradigm: decoupling intent classification from language generation. Intent routing would be handled by a dedicated, frozen neural classification model that produces exact, reproducible probability scores in milliseconds.

In real life, human speech can express multiple intents in a single breath (e.g., "I weighed 82kg this morning and I want to log my salmon lunch"). However, we knew we had to crawl before we could run. During Week 8, we focused exclusively on single-intent classification on single sentences. This allowed us to understand the underlying loss functions, benchmark multiple neural architectures, eliminate mutual exclusivity bugs, and design a pristine repository layout.

4 Classifier Architectures Evaluated

Before settling on a final model, we implemented and rigorously benchmarked four distinct classification architectures:

Figure 1: Comparison of the 4 classifier architectures evaluated during Week 8. Only Token Mean Pooling with independent Sigmoid heads satisfied our auditability and accuracy criteria.

Architecture 1: Rule-Based / RegEx Pattern Matching

Why we tried it: RegEx is instant (<0.05ms), requires zero training, has zero dependencies, and provides 100% transparent boolean audit trails.

Why it failed: Human speech is fluid and filled with speech-to-text artifacts. A user asking "salmon calorie count please" or "what's the energy profile of grilled salmon" failed because our rules expected "how many calories in...". Handling typos, conversational fluff ("um, can you tell me..."), and phrasing variations required an impossibly brittle web of manual regular expressions. Accuracy collapsed to 57.1%.

Architecture 2: TF-IDF + Linear Classifier (SVM / Logistic Regression)

Why we tried it: It takes under 0.5ms on CPU, has a tiny footprint (<2MB), and produces linear feature weights that can be inspected directly for audits.

Why it failed: Bag-of-words completely ignores word order and syntactic context. It fundamentally could not distinguish negation: "Give me cheese with lactose" versus "Give me cheese without lactose" produced nearly identical term vectors. Furthermore, out-of-vocabulary synonyms caused unpredictable score drops. Accuracy was limited to 71.4%.

Architecture 3: Transformer with [CLS] Pooling & Softmax

Why we tried it: A pre-trained Transformer text encoder (TinyBERT / DistilBERT) understands semantics, synonyms, and conversational variations natively.

Why it failed: We encountered two severe mathematical bottlenecks:

  • Softmax Mutual Exclusivity: Softmax forces all output probabilities to sum to 1.0 (Σ pi = 1.0). This has a dangerous side effect: if a user speaks complete nonsense or an out-of-domain query (e.g. "Tell me a joke about quantum physics"), Softmax is mathematically forced to assign 100% of the probability mass across the available internal labels, forcing a high-confidence false positive!
  • The [CLS] Token Bottleneck: Squeezing an entire sentence's contextual meaning solely into the first [CLS] token representation proved brittle on multi-clause sentences.

Architecture 4: BERT with Token Mean Pooling & Independent Sigmoid Heads

Why it succeeded: This was our breakthrough. We re-engineered the neural architecture with two fundamental changes:

  1. Token Mean Pooling: Instead of discarding token representations and relying solely on [CLS], we pool across all active tokens weighted by the attention mask:
    hpooled = Σ (hi × mi) / Σ mi
    where mi ∈ {0, 1} is the attention mask. This creates a dense, semantically rich sentence embedding.
  2. Independent Sigmoid Multi-Label Heads: We completely discarded Softmax in favor of independent Sigmoids:
    ŷc = σ(wcT z + bc) = 1 / (1 + e-(wcT z + bc))
    Every intent receives an independent score between 0.0 and 1.0. If a user enters out-of-domain noise, all heads naturally output near 0.0. When a valid intent is present, it fires cleanly above its calibrated threshold.

We trained the model with a Multi-Task Binary Cross-Entropy Loss with Label Smoothing across Level 1 global categories and Level 2 sub-intents:

Ljoint = 0.3 × BCEsmooth(ŷglobal, yglobal) + 0.7 × BCEsmooth(ŷsub, ysub)

At inference time, top-down hierarchy probability consistency is mathematically enforced:

Peffective(child) = P(child) × P(parent)

Exported to ONNX Opset 17, this architecture executes on our Orange Pi 5 CPU in just 4.15 milliseconds with 100% deterministic reproducibility.

Structuring the Classifier Repository

To ensure long-term maintainability, MLOps automation, and strict separation of concerns, we organized the classifier/ package in the VITA codebase into dedicated subsystems:

Subsystem Directory Primary Responsibilities Key Files
classifier/inference/ Runtime ONNX execution, phoneme stutter repair, quality gate, domain centroid gating, and decision policy. runtime.py, decision_policy.py, preprocessor.py
classifier/networks/ Pure PyTorch neural network definitions. Zero HTTP, ONNX, or dataset dependencies. architecture.py, heads.py, classifier.py, onnx_wrapper.py
classifier/dataset/ Dataset engineering, taxonomy definitions, synthetic generation, and deterministic train/val splits. taxonomy.json, dataset.json, dataset_builder.py
classifier/training/ PyTorch training harness, stratified loaders, multi-task BCE loss, threshold calibration, and ONNX export. train.py, losses.py, calibration.py, export.py
classifier/monitoring/ Online drift detection, Population Stability Index (PSI), and unknown intent tracking. drift_detector.py
classifier/server.py High-performance standalone FastAPI serving container exposing /classify and /health. server.py

The Complete Decoupled Voice Pipeline

With our BERT classifier established as the deterministic gatekeeper, the entire VITA edge voice architecture was restructured into six strictly bounded, auditable stages:

Figure 2: The complete 6-stage decoupled edge voice pipeline. Every component has a single, strictly audited responsibility.

Step-by-Step Execution Trace

Let's trace what happens when a user speaks: "How many calories and how much protein in 150 grams of cooked salmon?"

  1. STT Engine (faster-whisper INT8): Converts the 16kHz microphone stream into raw transcript text in ~250ms.
  2. BERT Intent Classifier (ONNX Runtime CPU): Evaluates the transcript in 4.12ms. Returns food_composition_lookup with 0.9642 confidence (Hierarchy: ciqual -> food_composition_lookup).
  3. Constrained LLM Slot Extraction (Qwen 2.5 1.5B): The LLM receives only the JSON schema for food_composition_lookup. It extracts {"food_query": "salmon", "preparation": "cooked", "portion_grams": 150.0}. Prompt size is minimized to ~80 tokens.
  4. Auditable Knowledge API: Queries ciqual.db with parameterized parameters. Resolves alim_code: 20005 ("Atlantic salmon, grilled or pan-fried"). Scaled facts: 324 kcal, 36.9g protein, 19.65g fat, 0.75g carbs. Time: 1.25ms.
  5. LLM Response Formulation (Qwen 2.5 1.5B): Injected with the verified facts, the LLM generates: "150 grams of cooked Atlantic salmon provides 324 calories and 36.9 grams of protein."
  6. TTS Engine (piper-tts ONNX): Synthesizes natural speech audio in ~145ms.

Total end-to-end compute latency: 270 milliseconds. The response is natural, fast, and 100% medically defensive.

The Verdict: Why We Decided on BERT

By the end of Week 8, the decision was unambiguous: we standardized on a fine-tuned BERT transformer (specifically DistilBERT / all-MiniLM-L6-v2 with Token Mean Pooling and Sigmoid heads, exported to ONNX).

BERT gave us everything generative LLMs failed to deliver for intent routing:

  • Mathematical Invariance: Zero temperature variance. Exact floating-point reproducibility across millions of requests.
  • Blazing Speed on Edge Hardware: Ingests text, tokenizes with WordPiece, and executes in 4.15 milliseconds on a single ARM CPU core of our Orange Pi 5.
  • Zero Prompt Bloat: By offloading intent routing to BERT, we stripped hundreds of lines of routing instructions and JSON schema definitions out of Qwen's system prompt.
  • Safe Out-of-Domain Rejection: Because independent Sigmoids do not force probabilities to sum to 1.0, unrelated chatter naturally scores near zero, protecting downstream databases from rogue queries.

Looking Ahead to Week 9

While our BERT classifier mastered single-intent sentences with 100% determinism, real conversational speech is rarely that simple. Users frequently combine thoughts: "I weighed 82kg this morning, and what is the carbon footprint of salmon?"

In Week 9, we would begin the challenging journey of scaling our single-intent model into a true multi-label multi-intent engine: generating massive synthetic training corpora, resolving compound utterances, and building automated data pipelines to train our classifier across complex nutritional taxonomies...
