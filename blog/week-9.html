Week 9: Training the BERT Intent Classifier: Concepts, Taxonomy, and MLOps Failures

In Week 8, we made a pivotal architectural choice: after discovering that generative LLMs suffered from unacceptable probabilistic drift when asked to route intents (a 9.6% drift rate over 1,000 trials), we decided to offload intent routing to a dedicated, frozen BERT transformer. This guaranteed mathematical determinism, sub-5ms CPU latency, and zero prompt bloat on our Orange Pi 5.

Selecting BERT was only the first step. In Week 9, we had to answer the real engineering question: How do you train this classifier from scratch? How do you turn raw nutritional concepts into a balanced, reproducible dataset? And how do you overcome the inevitable failures—from Docker CUDA driver hell to threshold miscalibration?

The Explicit Scope of Week 9: Single-Intent Mastery

To establish a rock-solid foundation, Week 9 focused strictly on single-intent classification. This included both single-sentence queries (e.g., "How much protein is in 100g of salmon?") and multi-sentence single-intent queries (e.g., "I am cooking dinner for my family tonight. What is the iron content of lentils?"). We deliberately constrained our model to one intent per interaction before attempting multi-intent decomposition.

Taxonomy, Concepts, and the Dataset Architecture

Rather than hand-crafting thousands of ad-hoc training strings, we designed a pure-Python, concept-driven dataset engineering framework located in classifier/dataset/. The framework translates high-level clinical and nutritional knowledge into thousands of stratified training examples.

Figure 1: Concept-driven synthetic dataset compiler. Decouples taxonomy, clinical concepts, and linguistic style templates into a deterministic generation pipeline.

1. The Hierarchical Taxonomy (taxonomy.json)

Our intent taxonomy operates on two strictly defined hierarchical levels:

  • Level 1 (Global Category Nodes):
    • ciqual: Factual nutritional, allergen, or environmental questions answerable from our local databases.
    • user_profile: Biometric tracking, body weight updates, dietary goals, and preferences.
    • meta: Assistant diagnostic capabilities, system status, and data source inquiries.
    • none: Out-of-domain requests, small talk, or ambient background noise.
  • Level 2 (Granular Sub-Intents): Sub-nodes mapped to their respective global parents, including food_composition_lookup, allergen_cross_referencing, data_trust_confidence_codes, and environmental_footprint_agribalyse. 
// classifier/taxonomy.json
{
  "version": "1.0.0",
  "level1_nodes": {
    "ciqual": { "display_name": "CIQUAL food data" },
    "user_profile": { "display_name": "User profile" },
    "meta": { "display_name": "System metadata" },
    "none": { "display_name": "Out of domain" }

2. Concept Definitions & Linguistic Style Variations

Instead of hardcoding full sentences, we defined abstract concepts in YAML. For instance, classifier/dataset/concepts/composition.yaml binds permissible entity types (foods, nutrients) while strictly forbidding cross-domain terms (allergens, eco-scores) to prevent semantic leakage:

# classifier/dataset/concepts/composition.yaml
macronutrient_and_energy_lookup:
  id: macronutrient_and_energy_lookup
  global_category: ciqual
  intent: food_composition_lookup
  seed_entities: [foods, nutrients]
  forbidden_entities: [food_allergens, environmental_lca]
  persona_coverage: [general, athlete, parent]
  style_coverage: [questions, commands, fragments, conversational, voice, typos, broken_grammar]

These concepts are then combined with style templates in classifier/dataset/templates/ to generate realistic speech patterns:

  • Direct Questions: "What is the energy value of {food}?"
  • Voice ASR Artifacts: "{food} calories content please"
  • Multi-Sentence Single Intent: "I just got back from a 5k run. How much potassium is in a banana?"

The Neural Architecture: Mean Pooling & Dual Heads

To classify utterances efficiently on edge hardware, we built ProductionHierarchicalClassifier in classifier/networks/classifier.py using PyTorch:

Figure 2: Production hierarchical classifier architecture. Uses attention-weighted mean pooling and dual classification heads trained with masked multi-task loss.

Live Code: Token Mean Pooling Implementation

Rather than using standard HuggingFace wrappers, our PyTorch module performs deterministic mean pooling over the active attention mask:

# classifier/networks/classifier.py
class ProductionHierarchicalClassifier(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, encoder: nn.Module, hidden_dim: int, num_global_classes: int, num_sub_classes: int):
        super().__init__()
        self.encoder = encoder
        self.global_head = GlobalHead(hidden_dim, num_global_classes)
        self.sub_head = SubIntentHead(hidden_dim, num_sub_classes)

    def _pool_features(self, encoder_output, attention_mask: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        # Extract last hidden state
        hidden = encoder_output.last_hidden_state if hasattr(encoder_output, "last_hidden_state") else encoder_output[0]
        # Attention mask weighted mean pooling
        mask = attention_mask.unsqueeze(-1).float()
        pooled = (hidden * mask).sum(dim=1) / (mask.sum(dim=1).clamp(min=1e-9))
        return pooled

    def forward(self, input_ids: torch.Tensor, attention_mask: torch.Tensor):
        raw_output = self.encoder(input_ids=input_ids, attention_mask=attention_mask)
        features = self._pool_features(raw_output, attention_mask)
        return ClassifierOutput(
            global_logits=self.global_head(features),
            sub_logits=self.sub_head(features)
        )

Engineering Post-Mortems: The 5 Major Failures

Building our training pipeline was far from smooth. We encountered five severe technical bottlenecks before achieving production certification:

Failure 1: The Docker CUDA Driver Mismatch Hell

The Incident: When launching our training container, PyTorch immediately crashed with:
RuntimeError: The NVIDIA driver on your system is too old (found 12020).

Root Cause: The host development machine was running NVIDIA Driver 535.183.01, which supports up to CUDA 11.8. However, default Docker PyTorch container wheels pulled CUDA 12.0 binaries, causing the CUDA C-runtime to abort on startup.

The Fix: We explicitly pinned the PyTorch wheel in classifier/Dockerfile to the official CUDA 11.8 distribution:

# classifier/Dockerfile
RUN pip install --no-cache-dir \
    torch==2.1.2+cu118 \
    torchvision==0.16.2+cu118 \
    --index-url https://download.pytorch.org/whl/cu118

Failure 2: The CPU Training Drag (4+ Minutes vs. 11 Seconds)

The Incident: While debugging the CUDA driver mismatch, we temporarily ran training on the host CPU. Training 25 epochs across 850 samples took over 4 minutes (244 seconds). Iteration ground to a halt.

The Solution: Once our CUDA 11.8 wheel was operational on our local NVIDIA GTX 1050 GPU, training throughput surged to 1,947 samples per second. The entire 25-epoch training loop completed in just 10.9 seconds, unlocking rapid experiment cycles.

Compute Hardware Training Throughput 25 Epochs Duration Development Impact
CPU Host (8-Core Ryzen) 87.1 samples/sec 244.0 seconds (4.1 min) Slow iteration, debugging bottleneck
GPU (NVIDIA GTX 1050 cu118) 1,947.2 samples/sec 10.9 seconds 22.4x speedup, rapid MLOps cycle

Failure 3: The [CLS] Token Context Bottleneck

The Incident: In multi-sentence queries where users provided conversational context before asking a question (e.g. "I just got back from a workout. How much protein is in chicken breast?"), initial models frequently classified the query as meta or none.

Root Cause: Squeezing the full sentence representation into the single first [CLS] token caused context dilution over longer sequences.

The Fix: Switching to Token Mean Pooling across the entire attention mask preserved semantic features across all clauses, immediately restoring 99%+ accuracy on multi-sentence queries.

Failure 4: Global Threshold Miscalibration

The Incident: Using a fixed global probability cutoff of 0.50 caused high false-negative rates on rare sub-intents (like data_trust_confidence_codes) while over-predicting dominant composition queries.

The Fix: We implemented classifier/training/calibration.py, which runs a deterministic coordinate search over validation probabilities to find per-label optimal thresholds:

threshold*(c) = argmaxt F1(c, t)    subject to    ECE ≤ 0.05
This automated calibration lifted our Macro F1 score from 89.9% to 97.6%.

Failure 5: Out-of-Domain Noise Saturation

The Incident: Users speaking ambient noise or out-of-domain queries caused false-positive triggers.

The Fix: Replaced Softmax with independent Sigmoids, added a dedicated Level 1 none category, and implemented sub-intent loss masking in SymmetricBCELoss so non-CIQUAL queries receive zero gradient updates on nutritional sub-intents.

Production Certification & Deliverables

By the end of Week 9, our single-intent classification engine achieved full production validation:

  • Evaluation Metrics: 99.8% F1 on Level 1 global categories, 98.6% Macro F1 on Level 2 sub-intents.
  • Edge Performance: 4.15ms CPU inference time via ONNX Runtime on the Orange Pi 5.
  • Single-Intent Invariant Verified: Both single-sentence and multi-sentence single-intent queries route with 100% determinism.

Looking Ahead to Week 10

Mastering single-intent queries in Week 9 gave us the rigorous foundation we needed. But human communication is inherently compound: users frequently bundle multiple requests into a single breath:

"I weigh 82kg this morning, and can you tell me if this pesto has peanuts and what its carbon footprint is?"

A single-intent classifier cannot decompose this sentence without dropping critical requests. In Week 10, we will take our biggest leap yet: expanding our architecture to handle multiple intents in a single sentence—building synthetic multi-intent generators, designing joint sigmoid decomposition, and deploying multi-label routing to our edge pipeline...
